## 1. Introduction
This module visualizes the 3D stress and strain tensor fields generated by localized arbitrary traction vectors on basic geometries (Cuboid, Cylinder, Sphere). The underlying computations are based on the theoretical frameworks of traction vectors, stress tensors, and small-strain kinematics derived in the course lectures, together with Hooke's law (Chapter 6) and linear superposition (Chapter 10).

---

## 2. Traction Vectors and Cauchy's Stress Theorem (Chapter 2)

### 2.1 Definition of the Traction Vector
When an internal section cuts a deformed body into parts A and B, part B exerts a distributed force on part A. On an infinitesimal area $\Delta A$ with an outward unit normal $\mathbf{n}$, the traction vector $\mathbf{t}$ is defined as (Ch. 2, Eq. 1):
$$ \mathbf{t}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{n}) = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \frac{\Delta \mathbf{F}}{\Delta A} $$

### 2.2 Cauchy's Tetrahedron Theorem
Using Newton's Second Law ($\sum \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt}$) on an infinitesimal tetrahedron, body forces ($\propto h^3$) and inertia terms vanish in the limit as $h \to 0$ (Ch. 2, Eqs. 7–11). This yields Cauchy's formula, relating the traction on an arbitrary plane $\mathbf{n}$ to the tractions on the coordinate planes:
$$ \mathbf{t}_n(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^3 \mathbf{t}_i(\mathbf{x}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{e}_i) $$

Using tensor notation (Ch. 2, Eq. 19), this defines the **Cauchy Stress Tensor** $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$:
$$ \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^3 \mathbf{t}_i(\mathbf{x}) \otimes \mathbf{e}_i \quad \text{such that} \quad \mathbf{t}_n(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}) \mathbf{n} $$

In matrix multiplication form, the traction vector is:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} t_x \\ t_y \\ t_z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{11} & \tau_{12} & \tau_{13} \\ \tau_{21} & \sigma_{22} & \tau_{23} \\ \tau_{31} & \tau_{32} & \sigma_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} n_x \\ n_y \\ n_z \end{bmatrix} $$

## 3. Resolving Normal and Shear Components (Chapter 4)

Once the traction vector $\mathbf{t}_n$ acting on a plane is determined, it is resolved into two orthogonal components: the normal stress $\sigma_{nn}$ and the shear stress $\tau$.

### 3.1 Normal Stress
The normal component of traction is the projection of $\mathbf{t}_n$ along the normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ (Ch. 4, Eq. 1):
$$ \sigma_{nn} = \mathbf{t}_n \cdot \mathbf{n} = (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}) \cdot \mathbf{n} = \sum_i \sum_j \sigma_{ij} n_j n_i $$

### 3.2 Shear Stress Magnitude
The shear stress acts tangentially to the plane. By applying the Pythagorean theorem to the total traction vector (Ch. 4, Eq. 27):
$$ \tau^2 = ||\mathbf{t}_n||^2 - \sigma_{nn}^2 = ||\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}||^2 - [(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}) \cdot \mathbf{n}]^2 $$
$$ \tau = \sqrt{|\mathbf{t}_n|^2 - \sigma_{nn}^2} $$

### 3.3 Principal Stresses and Planes
Principal planes are defined as the planes on which the normal traction component $\sigma_{nn}$ is extremized (maximized/minimized) subject to the constraint $\sum n_i^2 = 1$. Using Lagrange multipliers, this leads to the eigenvalue problem (Ch. 4, Eq. 13):
$$ [\boldsymbol{\sigma}][\mathbf{n}] = \lambda [\mathbf{n}] $$
The roots of the characteristic equation $-\lambda^3 + I_1 \lambda^2 - I_2 \lambda + I_3 = 0$ (Ch. 4, Eq. 75) yield the three principal stresses ($\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3$). 

On principal planes, the shear stress is identically zero. The global maximum shear stress occurs on planes oriented at $45^\circ$ to the principal axes, with magnitude (Ch. 4, Eq. 44):
$$ \tau_{\max} = \frac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_3}{2} $$

## 4. The Strain Tensor and Strain Matrix (Chapter 5)

### 4.1 Kinematics of Deformation
For a displacement field $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t) = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{X}$, assuming small displacement gradients $\left| \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial X_j} \right| \ll 1$ (Ch. 5, Eq. 26), the deformation gradient tensor $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I} + \nabla\mathbf{u}$ admits an additive decomposition (Ch. 5, Eq. 72):
$$ \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} + \mathbf{W} $$

### 4.2 The Infinitesimal Strain Tensor
The symmetric part is the infinitesimal strain tensor $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, which describes length and angle changes (Ch. 5, Eq. 95):
$$ \boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u} + \nabla\mathbf{u}^T) $$
Or in index notation (Ch. 5, Eq. 71):
$$ \epsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial X_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial X_i} \right) $$

The off-diagonal terms relate to the engineering shear strain by $\epsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}$.
The trace of the strain matrix yields the volumetric strain (Ch. 5, Eq. 69):
$$ \epsilon_V = \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) = \epsilon_{11} + \epsilon_{22} + \epsilon_{33} $$

### 4.3 Equivalence to the Stress Tensor
As shown in the lectures, the strain tensor $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ transforms exactly like the stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$. The longitudinal strain along a line element $\mathbf{n}$ is $\epsilon_n = \mathbf{n}^T [\boldsymbol{\epsilon}] \mathbf{n}$, directly mirroring the normal traction equation $\sigma_{nn} = \mathbf{n}^T [\boldsymbol{\sigma}] \mathbf{n}$.

## 5. Implementation of the 3D Field

Sign convention: tension positive. Each load has a local frame $(\mathbf{n}_x,\mathbf{n}_y,\mathbf{n}_z)$ with $z$ pointing **into** the body; $r^2=x^2+y^2$ and $R^2=r^2+z^2$. The physics lives in `physics.js` (pure functions, used by the browser) and is mirrored in `server.py` for the section plots.

### 5.1 Normal point load (Boussinesq)
For a force $P$ pushing into a half-space the full stress state in cylindrical components is

$$ \sigma_z = -\frac{3Pz^3}{2\pi R^5},\qquad \tau_{rz} = -\frac{3Prz^2}{2\pi R^5} $$
$$ \sigma_r = \frac{P}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1-2\nu}{R(R+z)} - \frac{3r^2z}{R^5}\right],\qquad \sigma_\theta = \frac{P(1-2\nu)}{2\pi}\left[\frac{z}{R^3} - \frac{1}{R(R+z)}\right] $$

The hoop stress $\sigma_\theta$ is **not** zero and is part of the tensor (it enters von Mises and the strain). The tensor is rotated to Cartesian axes before use. These stresses satisfy $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma=0$ (Chapter 3) and the force balance $\int\sigma_z\,dA=-P$ on every horizontal plane.

### 5.2 Tangential point load (Cerruti)
For a force along $x$ or $y$ the displacement field is the Cerruti solution. The stress is obtained as in Chapters 5–6: the strain is the symmetric gradient of the displacement, $\boldsymbol\epsilon=\tfrac12(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^T)$, then Hooke's law $\boldsymbol\sigma=\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\epsilon)\mathbf I+2G\boldsymbol\epsilon$. The Lamé constant $\lambda=E\nu/[(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)]$ diverges as $\nu\to0.5$, so $\nu$ is capped at $0.499$ in this formula.

### 5.3 Distributed loads and superposition
The painted patch is represented by point loads at its vertices, each with an equivalent-depth offset $a$ ($z_{\text{eff}}=z+a$) that removes the singularity under a point. Because the equations are linear, the **tensors** of all point loads and of separately saved load cases are added (after rotation to global axes), and principal stresses, von Mises stress and strain are computed from the **summed tensor** (never by taking the maximum of scalars).

### 5.4 Quantities displayed
From the total tensor:

| Map | Definition |
|---|---|
| Normal stress | $\max(|\sigma_1|,|\sigma_3|)$ from the principal stresses (closed-form eigenvalues of the stress matrix, Ch. 4) |
| Max shear | $\tau_{\max}=(\sigma_1-\sigma_3)/2$ |
| Von Mises | $\sqrt{\tfrac12[(\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy})^2+\ldots]+3(\tau_{xy}^2+\tau_{yz}^2+\tau_{zx}^2)}$ |
| Strain | Frobenius norm of $\boldsymbol\epsilon=[(1+\nu)\boldsymbol\sigma-\nu\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\sigma)\mathbf I]/E$ (Hooke's law) |

Poisson's ratio follows the material (steel 0.30, aluminium 0.33, concrete 0.20, polymer 0.38, rubber 0.49). Displacements are drawn with an adjustable exaggeration factor; the scale does not depend on $E$, so stiffness ratios between materials are preserved.

### 5.5 Full-surface pressure
Chapter 2 shows that a pressure $p$ on the **entire** boundary gives the hydrostatic state $\boldsymbol\sigma=-p\,\mathbf I$ everywhere. The app uses this only when a normal pressure covers at least 99% of the surface area (area, not vertex count). Below that, the field is the pure half-space solution.

### 5.6 Section analytics
The drawer under the viewport plots a cut through the body on the plane of your choice. The server evaluates the same tensor (frames, equivalent depth and superposition as above) on an 80 × 80 grid and returns four maps, matching the 3D view: normal stress, max shear, von Mises and strain. Points outside the body are blank. The mask uses the true shape (box, cylinder with its axis along $y$, or sphere), and the slice position is limited to the body along the slice axis.

## 7. Finite Element Method (beyond the syllabus)
A separate tab solves the same governing equations numerically: the body is cut into elements, the displacement inside each is interpolated from nodal values, strain follows as $\boldsymbol\epsilon=\mathbf B\mathbf u_e$ (Ch. 5), stress as $\boldsymbol\sigma=\mathbf D\boldsymbol\epsilon$ (Ch. 6), and the element stiffness $\mathbf k_e=\int\mathbf B^T\mathbf D\,\mathbf B\,dV$ comes from strain energy (Ch. 10). Assembling all elements gives $\mathbf K\mathbf u=\mathbf f$.

| Problem | Elements | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Tapered bar | 2-node bar | exact integral of $P/EA$ |
| Cantilever beam | Hermite beam | $PL^3/3EI$, $wL^4/8EI$ |
| Plane-stress cantilever | CST triangle, Q4 quadrilateral | Euler–Bernoulli and Timoshenko |
| Plate with a hole | CST, Q4 (quarter model) | Kirsch, $K_t=3$ |
| 3D block with a patch load | 8-node brick (quarter model) | half-space (Boussinesq/Newmark) |

Unlike the half-space solution, the 3D block has real free faces and a base, so it enforces $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf 0$ on the sides. The 3D block is solved matrix-free by conjugate gradients (numpy only). Details and the concept primer are in `docs/fem_primer.tex`.

## 8. Verification by Limiting Cases
Every component was tested against closed forms and limiting cases (hydrostatic, uniaxial and pure-shear states; zero load; $\nu\to0.5$; very thin and very stubby members; full coverage; extreme inputs). The tests are not kept in the project.

| Component | Checks | What was verified |
|---|---|---|
| `physics.js` | 54 | invariants over 12 decades of stress; $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma=0$ for Boussinesq and Cerruti; stress equals Hooke of the displacement gradient; $\int\sigma_z\,dA=-P$; free surface; far field; singularities |
| `server.py` | 40 | eigen-decomposition against numpy; Cauchy relation; linearity and superposition; symmetry; masks; JS versus server agreement to $2\times10^{-4}$ for four load orientations |
| Simulators | 110 | beam (SS, cantilever, UDL, moment, Timoshenko limits), shaft, bar, Lamé, thermal, failure envelopes, Castigliano, Euler and Johnson |
| Simulators, random | 22 500 inputs | no exception, no NaN and no infinity except the safety factor with no load |
| FEM | 43 | patch test, closed forms, equilibrium, uniform-pressure block, convergence toward the half-space, 116 extreme inputs |
| 3D tab | 94 cases | every shape, material, load type and map with painted loads |

**Defects found and corrected in this round**

1. *Torsion, energy tab:* the closed-form displacement and work were off by a factor of $10^6$ (unit slip); the status check had skipped torsion. Fixed.
2. *Short columns:* the Euler stress exceeded yield (up to $4\times10^4$ MPa) and the safety factor used it. A Johnson parabola now governs when $\lambda<\pi\sqrt{E/\sigma_y}$.
3. *Principal stresses:* a fixed threshold treated tiny non-hydrostatic stress states as hydrostatic. The test is now relative to the stress scale.
4. *$\nu=0.5$:* the Lamé constant divided by zero (infinite stress in JS, `ZeroDivisionError` on the server). $\nu$ is capped at 0.499 there.
5. *Section endpoint:* a resolution below 2 crashed (now clamped to 2–400) and a non-unit force direction scaled the load (now normalised).
6. *Display:* an infinite safety factor showed as "–"; it now shows "∞". Strains above 1% flag that small-strain theory is exceeded.

## 9. Limitations
- Linear elastic, small strain: rubber under load can exceed 100% strain in the 3D tab, where the numbers are only indicative (the readout says so).
- The half-space field assumes an infinite body: it leaves stress on the sides and base of a finite one. The FEM block tab quantifies this (side-face $\sigma_x$ about 0.4% of $q$ in the FEM, against about 2.6% for the half-space at the default block).
- A partial patch load is not in equilibrium in the 3D tab (there are no supports); displacements are referenced to the bottom centre.
- FEM: plane stress only; CST and Q4 are stiff in bending until refined (coarse slender Q4 about 11% too stiff, CST about 46%); 8-node bricks are stiff for nearly incompressible materials; surface stresses are extrapolated and least accurate.
- Beams are integrated numerically on 401 points. The column simulator assumes a straight, concentrically loaded column.
